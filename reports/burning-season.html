Two seasons, not one

Detections by calendar month, summed over six years. The forest-fire season in March and April dwarfs the stubble burning in October and November, and the monsoon months are close to empty. An earlier version of this page looked only at January, October, November and December — which is how the larger season went unseen.

Detections by month, year over year

The four months the original window covered, each followed across six years. November sits above the other three and falls after 2021; October declines in every year of the series. Neither is the year's peak — March is.

Rain stops the burning

Detections in the belt on the day after rain, against the day after a dry day, measured at three weather stations. Amritsar and Patiala sit inside the belt; Delhi is 250 km outside it and was the only option before those two were added. All three agree, which is a stronger claim than any one of them makes alone.

What survives once the calendar is removed

Fire and weather both follow the seasons, so a raw correlation between them largely measures the time of year. With six complete years it can be subtracted: build a day-of-year climatology, take the anomaly — how far each day sits from its own normal — and correlate those instead. What is left is the part the calendar does not explain.

raw
deseasonalised

Humidity is the real driver, keeping 58% of its strength. Rainfall keeps about a third — still real, but far less than it looked. And temperature does the opposite: raw it reads +0.001, apparently nothing, because two opposing seasonal effects cancel. Remove the season and it becomes +0.157. A four-month window could not have found that.

Does the smoke reach the cities?

The obvious next question, and the data answers it — just not the way you would hope. Each point is one city: how far it sits from the centre of the burning belt, against how strongly its daily PM2.5 tracks daily belt detections through October and November. If smoke transport drove the relationship, near cities would sit high and far ones low.

It does not hold. Mumbai, 1,302 km away, correlates as strongly as anything in the belt itself. Hollow markers show the same correlation after the day-of-year climatology is removed: every city collapses to near zero, the two inside the belt to −0.004 and −0.012. The entire apparent relationship was the shared calendar.

One more reason not to read this as evidence

The PM2.5 here is CAMS reanalysis, not a ground measurement — and CAMS is fed by the Global Fire Assimilation System, which ingests MODIS and VIIRS active-fire observations: the same two sensors supplying the detection counts on the other axis. The model was told where the fires were. Even the raw correlation was partly circular by construction.

Method & caveats

  • A detection is not a fire. Each row is one satellite pixel flagged as actively burning at overpass. One fire spanning several pixels counts several times, and a fire between overpasses is missed entirely.
  • Two sensors, different footprints. VIIRS SNPP resolves 375 m and supplies 1,280,086 of the rows; MODIS resolves 1 km and supplies 222,640. Counts are not directly comparable between them.
  • Cloud cover suppresses counts. A lower number can mean less burning or more cloud. The decline shown here is real in the data, but attributing it to enforcement, weather or crop timing needs work this chart does not do.
  • Archive, not near-real-time. Pulled from the Standard Processing archives via backfill_firms.py, which reprocess and slightly revise the NRT feed.
  • 738 distinct days appear where 731 were expected: timestamps are UTC, so late-evening overpasses spill into the next calendar day.
