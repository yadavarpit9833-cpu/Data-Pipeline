Rain stops the burning

Detections in the belt on the day after rain, against the day after a dry day, measured at three weather stations. Amritsar and Patiala sit inside the belt; Delhi is 250 km outside it and was the only option before those two were added. All three agree, which is a stronger claim than any one of them makes alone.

The effect is weakest at Delhi’s 0.14 ratio and strongest there only because Delhi records the fewest wet days of the three. Amritsar, inside the belt and with the most rain, gives 0.20 — so treat that as the honest figure.

Does it hold every year?

Rank correlation between daily rainfall and daily detections, per station per year. A correlation is only worth trusting if its sign stays put; bars left of the line are negative, meaning more rain went with fewer fires.

Patiala is negative in all six years. Delhi has no bar for 2024 because no rain fell there at all that October or November, so the correlation is undefined rather than missing. Amritsar’s 2022 and 2024 sit near zero — the signal is directionally consistent but not uniformly strong.

Method & caveats

  • A detection is not a fire. Each row is one satellite pixel flagged as actively burning at overpass. One fire spanning several pixels counts several times, and a fire between overpasses is missed entirely.
  • Two sensors, different footprints. VIIRS SNPP resolves 375 m and supplies 1,280,086 of the rows; MODIS resolves 1 km and supplies 222,640. Counts are not directly comparable between them.
  • Cloud cover suppresses counts. A lower number can mean less burning or more cloud. The decline shown here is real in the data, but attributing it to enforcement, weather or crop timing needs work this chart does not do.
  • Archive, not near-real-time. Pulled from the Standard Processing archives via backfill_firms.py, which reprocess and slightly revise the NRT feed.
  • 738 distinct days appear where 731 were expected: timestamps are UTC, so late-evening overpasses spill into the next calendar day.
